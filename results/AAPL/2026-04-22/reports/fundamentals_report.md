Apple Inc. (AAPL) Fundamental Report  
Date: 2026-04-22

Apple remains one of the highest-quality mega-cap franchises in global equities, with exceptional profitability, durable cash generation, an unmatched capital return program, and a balance sheet that—despite headline leverage and negative working capital—still reflects substantial financial flexibility. The latest quarterly financial history available through the dataset suggests that Apple’s core business continues to compound earnings at an elite scale, though valuation already embeds high expectations and the company is trading at premium multiples relative to many large-cap peers.

Company profile and business quality
Apple operates in consumer electronics, software, and services, sitting within the Technology sector and Consumer Electronics industry. Its business model is unusually powerful because it combines premium hardware, ecosystem lock-in, a large installed base, recurring services monetization, and supply-chain scale. This creates a financial profile that traders should view differently from most hardware companies: Apple is not just cyclical device revenue, but a platform company with hardware as the anchor of a broader monetization engine.

As of 2026-04-22, Apple’s market capitalization is about $3.91 trillion, confirming it remains one of the largest and most systemically important public companies in the world. Its beta of 1.109 indicates the stock generally moves a bit more than the market, though the underlying business is still relatively resilient compared with lower-quality growth names. The stock trades near the upper portion of its 52-week range, with a 52-week high of 288.62 and low of 193.25. The 50-day moving average of 260.27 is above the 200-day average of 252.71, indicating constructive medium-term technical momentum layered on top of strong fundamentals.

Valuation and market expectations
Apple trades at a trailing P/E of 33.65 and forward P/E of 28.36, with a PEG ratio of 2.46. This is not a cheap stock on conventional valuation terms. The market is assigning a premium multiple for earnings durability, capital returns, and balance-sheet quality. Traders should interpret this as a stock where earnings beats and guidance strength can support further upside, but any indication of slowing revenue growth, margin compression, regulatory pressure, or a softer product cycle can trigger sharper multiple compression than would occur in a lower-multiple value stock.

Price-to-book is extremely high at 44.38, but this metric is not especially useful for Apple because years of aggressive buybacks have materially reduced reported equity. That effect also distorts ROE upward. Apple’s reported return on equity is 152%, which looks extraordinary but is flattered by a relatively small equity base rather than purely reflecting incremental economics. Return on assets at 24.38% is more informative and still excellent, underscoring how efficiently Apple monetizes its asset base.

Profitability overview
Apple’s trailing twelve-month revenue stands at about $435.6 billion, gross profit at $206.2 billion, EBITDA at $152.9 billion, and net income at $117.8 billion. These are remarkable absolute numbers, but what matters more for traders is how much profit is retained from each dollar of sales:

- Profit margin: 27.0%
- Operating margin: 35.4%

These margins are elite for a company of this scale. They indicate that Apple continues to command pricing power, maintain a favorable product mix, and keep operating costs under control even while investing heavily in research and development. For traders, operating margin stability is especially important. A company this large can still create significant earnings upside if revenue growth and gross margin hold while opex grows slower than sales.

Fine-grained observations:
1. Seasonal strength remains powerful. The December quarter remains Apple’s standout period, and the step-up from September to December 2025 is very large. This is typical for Apple, but the absolute scale matters: revenue moved from about $102.5 billion to $143.8 billion, while net income rose from $27.5 billion to $42.1 billion. That demonstrates excellent drop-through from revenue into earnings.
2. Year-over-year December quarter comparison is favorable. Revenue rose from $124.3 billion in 2024-12 to $143.8 billion in 2025-12, while net income rose from $36.3 billion to $42.1 billion. That is a strong signal that the most recent major product cycle and/or services monetization environment was supportive.
3. Operating expenses are rising, but not alarmingly. R&D increased from $8.268 billion in 2024-12 to $10.887 billion in 2025-12. This reflects continued investment in platform development, silicon, AI/software capabilities, and ecosystem initiatives. The key point is that this investment is occurring while margins remain very strong, implying Apple is funding future growth internally without sacrificing present profitability.
4. Tax rates remain controlled. Quarterly tax rates generally ranged from roughly 14.7% to 17.5%, which supports earnings quality and predictability.

What this means for traders:
1. Apple’s earnings are backed by real cash conversion. The company is not dependent on accounting adjustments to manufacture profitability.
2. Capital intensity remains low relative to cash generation. Capex in most quarters runs only around $2.4–3.5 billion, which is very manageable against operating cash flow. This keeps free cash flow margins high.
3. Buybacks remain massive and consistent. Apple repurchased roughly $20–25.9 billion of stock per quarter in recent periods. This is material support for EPS growth and can cushion downside during broad market weakness.
4. Dividends are easily covered. Dividend payments of around $3.8–3.9 billion per quarter are only a small fraction of free cash flow.
5. Apple is still paying down debt selectively, not aggressively enough to impair shareholder returns but enough to manage maturity risk and capital structure.

Share count reduction and EPS support
One of the most important underlying drivers for Apple’s stock performance continues to be share count reduction.

Ordinary shares declined from about 15.038 billion at 2024-12-31 to 14.698 billion at 2025-12-31. Diluted average shares also fell meaningfully across the quarterly income statement periods. This matters because:
- It mechanically boosts EPS even if net income growth slows.
- It increases each remaining shareholder’s claim on future cash flows.
- It acts as a structural support to per-share valuation metrics.

For traders, this means EPS resilience may remain stronger than pure revenue growth would suggest. Apple can still produce respectable per-share growth in a moderate top-line environment because the buyback engine is so large.

Balance sheet analysis
Apple’s balance sheet requires nuanced interpretation. On the surface, some metrics appear weak:
- Debt-to-equity: 102.63
- Current ratio: 0.974
- Working capital: negative in all recent quarters

However, these figures are less alarming than they would be for most companies.

Latest quarter: 2025-12-31
- Total assets: $379.3 billion
- Total liabilities: $291.1 billion
- Stockholders’ equity: $88.2 billion
- Total debt: $90.5 billion
- Net debt: $45.2 billion
- Cash, cash equivalents, and short-term investments: $66.9 billion
- Current assets: $158.1 billion
- Current liabilities: $162.4 billion
- Working capital: negative $4.3 billion

Important balance-sheet insights:
1. Negative working capital is part of Apple’s model, not necessarily distress. Apple collects cash quickly, has enormous supplier leverage, and manages inventory tightly. Negative or thin working capital in this context reflects bargaining power and cash-cycle efficiency more than liquidity weakness.
2. Liquidity is still ample. Cash plus short-term investments near $67 billion gives Apple substantial near-term flexibility.
3. Net debt is moderate relative to earnings and free cash flow. With annual free cash flow above $100 billion, net debt near $45 billion is very manageable.
4. Equity has grown from $66.8 billion at 2024-12-31 to $88.2 billion at 2025-12-31, despite ongoing buybacks. That suggests retained earnings and valuation adjustments have more than offset some capital return effects over this interval.
5. Long-term debt declined from roughly $84.0 billion at 2024-12-31 to $76.7 billion at 2025-12-31. This is constructive and shows Apple is not levering up recklessly while repurchasing shares.

Asset composition and operating discipline
Apple’s asset base is diversified and conservatively managed:
- Investments and advances remained substantial, around $77.9 billion at 2025-12-31.
- Net PPE increased to about $50.2 billion, indicating continued infrastructure and operational investment.
- Inventory remained low at $5.875 billion relative to revenue scale, showing efficient supply-chain management.
- Receivables reached $70.3 billion at 2025-12-31, up from lower mid-year levels, which is normal given seasonality and stronger sales volume.

The low inventory intensity is especially notable. Apple can generate nearly $144 billion in quarterly revenue while carrying less than $6 billion in inventory, which speaks to turnover efficiency and demand planning discipline.

Dividend profile
Apple’s dividend yield is only 0.39%, so this is not an income stock in the traditional sense. The dividend is better viewed as a signal of confidence and capital discipline than as the main shareholder return mechanism. The real yield to shareholders is buyback-driven. Traders focused on total capital return should care much more about free cash flow and repurchase pace than the nominal dividend yield.

Key strengths for traders
1. Elite profitability: gross, operating, and net margins remain exceptional for a company of Apple’s size.
2. Massive free cash flow: over $100 billion TTM gives management extraordinary strategic flexibility.
3. Buyback support: quarterly repurchases above $20 billion create ongoing EPS support.
4. Strong recent December-quarter growth: this reduces fears of stagnation and reinforces confidence in demand durability.
5. Manageable leverage: debt looks large in absolute terms but is very serviceable relative to cash flow.
6. Ecosystem durability: services and installed base economics likely support premium multiples.

Key risks and watchpoints
1. Premium valuation: at ~33.6x trailing earnings and ~28.4x forward earnings, Apple leaves less room for disappointment.
2. Margin sensitivity: if product mix weakens or competition/regulation pressures services economics, high margins may compress.
3. Slower top-line periods could pressure sentiment more than fundamentals: because the stock is priced as a quality compounder, even modest deceleration may be punished.
4. Current ratio below 1 and negative working capital could be misread by the market during risk-off periods, even if fundamentally manageable.
5. Dependence on continued execution: Apple’s valuation premium assumes continued successful monetization of ecosystem, services, hardware refresh cycles, and innovation initiatives.

Bottom line
Apple’s fundamentals remain very strong. The company combines scale, profitability, free cash flow, and capital return in a way few companies can match. The financial statements show not just resilience but real operational power: strong seasonal upside, year-over-year improvement in the latest major quarter, disciplined opex growth, robust free cash flow conversion, ongoing debt management, and aggressive share reduction.

From a trader’s perspective, Apple still looks like a high-quality compounder rather than a deep-value setup. The core bullish argument is not that the stock is cheap, but that the business quality is so high and cash generation so durable that premium valuation can remain justified if execution stays intact. The main caution is that much of this quality is already recognized by the market, so near-term upside may depend on continued earnings beats, favorable guidance, or catalysts around product cycles and services growth.

In summary: Apple’s business fundamentals are excellent, its balance sheet is healthier than superficial leverage ratios imply, and its cash flow engine remains one of the most powerful in public markets. The principal debate for traders is less about business quality and more about whether the current valuation sufficiently compensates for execution and multiple-compression risk.
